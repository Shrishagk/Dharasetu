Engine 1: Spatial matching — treat it as a graph problem, not a distance problem

Every naive implementation of "spatial matching" does nearest-centroid or IoU-overlap matching: take a parcel from the drone-ORI layer, find the closest polygon in the cadastral layer, call it a match. This breaks constantly in real cadastral data because parcels get subdivided, merged, or redrawn at different scales between epochs — a single 2020 revenue plot might correspond to three 2026 municipal sub-plots, and centroid distance has no way to express that.

The actual research solution, and what should be the core of this engine, is to stop matching individual geometries and instead match graphs:

Convert each dataset's features into nodes of a graph — each node embeds shape (morphology), absolute position, and relative position to its neighbours, not just its own coordinates.

A 2025 paper on this exact problem (matching map features extracted by deep learning from different sensor sources) builds (cite index="121-1">a graph neural network approach — KRAFT — that constructs a knowledge graph of spatial relationships, aligns entities through graph alignment and geospatial encoding, and merges unmatched features through mixed-integer linear programming rather than heuristic buffering.</cite>

This directly solves the many-to-many subdivision/merger problem: a 2011-era but still-standard technique for exactly this uses (cite index="119-1">a global optimisation approach where road networks segment candidate regions, spatial and attribute similarity is computed with entropy-weighted criteria, and the actual assignment is solved as a Hungarian-algorithm allocation problem — achieving 97.2% precision and 97% recall on building-entity matching,</cite> far outperforming greedy nearest-neighbour approaches.

For cases with heavy positional distortion between sources (a hand-digitized 1990s revenue map vs. a 2026 CORS-corrected drone survey), genetic-algorithm and integer-linear-programming formulations exist specifically because the problem is combinatorial, not distance-based — (cite index="117-1">shape-context descriptors combined with genetic algorithms have matched up to 40% of polygons even where geometric attributes differed significantly between sources,</cite> and more recent work frames the whole thing as (cite index="120-1">an integer linear program with a divide-and-conquer tiling scheme to keep large-scale (e.g., ULB or state-wide) conflation computationally tractable.</cite>

The practical build choice that makes this a real USP, not a paper reference: don't train a shape-embedding network from scratch — a hackathon team doesn't have the labeled Indian cadastral data to do that well. Instead, use a pretrained geospatial foundation model as the encoder backbone. Models like (cite index="150-1">IBM-NASA's Prithvi, pretrained on over a terabyte of multi-temporal satellite imagery via self-supervised masked-autoencoding, transfer to downstream tasks with up to 90% less labeled data than training from scratch,</cite> and by 2026 there are (cite index="153-1">three production-ready options — Prithvi-EO-2.0, Clay v1.5, and Google's AlphaEarth Foundations — all open-weight and usable as drop-in embedding extractors for similarity search and change detection without training a model yourself.</cite> Use one of these to embed each building/parcel footprint, then run change-detection-style Siamese comparison on the embeddings — this is a mature technique achieving (cite index="137-1">F1 scores exceeding 90% on standard benchmarks (LEVIR-CD, OSCD) using paired-image Siamese networks that share weights and compare feature maps rather than raw pixels.</cite>

So Engine 1, concretely: feature graph construction → foundation-model embeddings (not trained from scratch) → GNN-based relational matching → Hungarian/ILP optimal assignment for many-to-many resolution.

Engine 2: Semantic attribute mapping — this is the one nobody has actually solved for cadastral data yet

This is the strongest part of your USP because the enabling technique is barely a year old. The problem: a Karnataka revenue record has "Khata No.", a Tamil Nadu municipal layer has "Patta No.", a utility database has "Consumer Property ID", and NGDRS has yet another field name — often in different scripts. Every platform I found last time (FME, NGDRS, GeoMedia Fusion) solves this with a human sitting down and authoring a static crosswalk table per state, per department. That doesn't scale to 152 ULBs today and ~4,912 eventually.

The 2024–2026 research answer is retrieval-augmented LLM schema matching, and it's specifically designed to replace exactly this kind of manual crosswalk:

Rather than one brittle LLM call, the state of the art generates candidate matches first (via embedding similarity), then uses an LLM as a reranker that reads field names, descriptions, and sample values together. (cite index="124-1">ReMatch converts target schemas into retrievable documents and filters candidates by semantic similarity before LLM reranking; KG-RAG4SM goes further by grounding the match against an external knowledge graph rather than relying on field-name similarity alone; Matchmaker iteratively refines candidates using confidence scores across multiple rounds of LLM feedback.</cite>

This matters a lot for your case specifically: a 2025 paper on unified LLM schema matching found real multi-table enterprise schema alignment (exactly your NGDRS + municipal + utility + revenue situation) needs (cite index="127-1">a two-stage strategy — a "rollup" step that consolidates semantically related columns into higher-order concepts, then a "drilldown" step that re-expands them for fine-grained column mapping</cite> — because a flat one-to-one field comparison misses that "Khata No." and "Survey No." both roll up to the same higher concept ("parcel identifier") even though they aren't identical fields.

The honest caveat, also from the research: raw LLM calls are unreliable on their own. A 2025 paper on scalable schema mapping found (cite index="123-1">LLM outputs are sensitive to input phrasing and structure, requiring sampling-and-aggregation strategies rather than trusting a single response, and that repeated LLM calls carry real computational cost that needs prefiltering by data type to control.</cite> Build this in from day one — it's a legitimate engineering answer to a legitimate limitation, and saying so in your pitch reads as more credible than pretending LLMs just work out of the box.

The piece that makes this specifically defensible for land records (not generic enterprise data): ground the LLM's target schema in the Land Administration Domain Model (LADM/ISO 19152) instead of an arbitrary internal schema. This isn't hypothetical — Colombia has already built and deployed almost exactly this for a decentralized multi-agency land system: (cite index="158-1">a graph-based knowledge representation built on an LADM-grounded ontology, specifically designed to integrate heterogeneous distributed data — tables, shapefiles, geodatabases, and live WFS services — from separate cadastral agencies while preventing data isolation.</cite> Their pipeline concretely (cite index="164-1">transforms each agency's data into RDF using a shared ontology network, producing integrated knowledge graphs that work as, in their words, a common language between humans and machines.</cite> For India, that means: LLM proposes the match ("Khata No." → LADM's spatial_unit.id), the knowledge graph validates it's semantically consistent with LADM's Party/Administrative Unit/Spatial Unit structure, and — crucially — this works across Hindi/Tamil/Telugu/English field labels because embedding models and LLMs handle cross-lingual semantic similarity natively, which a static string-matching crosswalk never could.

So Engine 2, concretely: embed field name + description + sample values → retrieve candidate LADM-ontology matches → LLM reranks with rollup/drilldown for multi-table cases → validate against the knowledge graph → emit mapping with per-field confidence.

The layer that makes both engines trustworthy: confidence scoring via conformal prediction

This is the single most underexploited opportunity in the entire landscape I found last time. Every platform either has no confidence score at all, or a raw, uncalibrated softmax number that doesn't actually mean "there's a 90% chance this match is correct." The fix that's statistically rigorous rather than hand-wavy is conformal prediction:

(cite index="141-1">Conformal prediction uses past calibration data to determine precise, statistically valid confidence levels for new predictions, relying only on a weak exchangeability assumption rather than any specific distributional assumption — and it's model-agnostic, so it wraps around your GNN and your LLM matcher identically.</cite>

Concretely, (cite index="142-1">if you specify a 95% confidence level, the conformal predictor guarantees the true match is in its output set 95% of the time — and when a prediction doesn't reach that confidence level, it can emit a null result or flag the case for human intervention automatically,</cite> which is exactly the auto-merge/human-review fork in the diagram above — it's not a heuristic threshold you tune by guessing, it's a mathematically calibrated one.

This genuinely is white space: a 2024 review of Earth observation ML found (cite index="147-1">only 22.5% of EO datasets incorporate any uncertainty information at all, with unreliable methods prevalent where it exists,</cite> and a separate paper notes plainly that (cite index="142-1">conformal prediction has yet to be widely adopted within GeoAI despite its maturity elsewhere.</cite> You'd be applying a well-proven statistical method to a domain that hasn't picked it up yet — that's a real, citable novelty claim, not marketing language.

There's a wrinkle worth knowing (and worth mentioning in your pitch, because catching it yourself is a credibility signal): land/property data is spatially autocorrelated, so naive conformal prediction miscalibrates — confidence sets end up too wide in some areas and too narrow in others. Research on exactly this problem in property valuation found (cite index="144-1">spatially-weighted conformal prediction, calibrating on a locally-weighted version of the nonconformity scores, produces confidence sets that stay properly calibrated across different geographic regions</cite> — directly transferable to your case, since match confidence in dense urban cores (Chandigarh sectors) will behave differently than in sparse peri-urban NAKSHA sites.

Why this combination is a genuine, defensible USP

Going back to the landscape I found earlier: FME does rule-based ETL, Esri Parcel Fabric does rule-based topology/COGO correction, NGDRS does document workflow, and even RMSI's utility-network conflation work is a specialized, non-cadastral application of similar ideas. Nowhere did I find a system that combines all three of: (1) graph/GNN-based many-to-many geometric matching, (2) LADM-grounded LLM+knowledge-graph semantic mapping, and (3) conformal-prediction-based confidence scoring, applied specifically to cadastral integration. Each piece individually is 2024–2026 research; the combination, for this exact problem, doesn't appear to exist in production anywhere.

It's also genuinely buildable in a hackathon timeframe because it leans on pretrained, open components rather than requiring you to train anything large from scratch:

A worked example for your demo

Take a single property in a NAKSHA pilot ULB. The revenue department's record says Khasra_No: 142/2. The municipal GIS layer, for the same physical plot, says PropertyID: WD3-0891. The drone-ORI-derived footprint has no attribute at all, just a polygon. Your pipeline: Engine 1 matches the polygon to the nearest cadastral geometry using GNN embeddings (handling the case where this plot was actually split from a larger 2019 parcel) with, say, 0.94 confidence. Engine 2's LLM, grounded in the LADM ontology, proposes that Khasra_No and PropertyID both map to the same spatial_unit.id concept, with the knowledge graph confirming both fields' data types and contexts are consistent with that class — 0.89 confidence. Conformal prediction wraps both scores into a calibrated joint confidence for the merged record; if it clears your calibrated threshold, the unified record auto-publishes with a full audit trail of which source contributed which field; if not, it routes to a revenue officer's review queue with both candidate values shown side by side, not a blind auto-merge.

That worked example, plus the three cited techniques, is a much stronger pitch than "we'll use AI for matching" — it's specific enough that a judge familiar with GIS will recognize you've actually engaged with why this is hard, not just proposed a black box.
